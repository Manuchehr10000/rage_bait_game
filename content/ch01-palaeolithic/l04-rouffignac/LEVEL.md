# Chapter 1 · Level 4 · Rouffignac

Rouffignac-Saint-Cernin-de-Reilhac, Dordogne. The cave of a hundred mammoths: more than
eight kilometres of dry, wide, flat-floored galleries in a limestone full of flint, and
the largest decorated cave in the Périgord. About 250 figures, of which **158 are
mammoths** — seventy per cent of the animals in the cave — with ten woolly rhinoceros,
horses, bison, ibex, and four human figures. Nearly all of it is black manganese drawing
and engraving, Middle Magdalenian, c. 13,000 BP.

It has been visited for a very long time. François de Belleforest described it in the
*Cosmographie universelle* of 1575 — "paintings in several places, and the traces of
various kinds of beasts" — and four centuries of visitors have left their names on the
walls, some of them straight across the animals. The art was not recognised for what it
was until **1956**, when Louis-René Nougier and Romain Robert published it. Breuil
authenticated it on 17 July 1956; the prehistorian Sévérin Blanc called the drawings
fakes; an international commission ended what Nougier and Robert themselves called *la
guerre des mammouths*. Since **1959** the cave has been visited by **electric train**,
about a kilometre in and an hour there and back, and the train carries the lighting.

And the thing this level is built on: at the far end of the visit the train stops at the
**Great Ceiling** — sixty-five animals drawn on a roof so low the artist lay on his back
to do it — which hangs over a shaft to the lower galleries. **The hole was filled in and
the floor lowered** so that visitors could stand there and look up.

## What this level is for

Level 4 escalates. The first three levels each lied in a different way; this one changes
what the tourist is doing. He is not walking through a cave; he is walking up the track
of a scheduled train, and the train is behind him.

> Cap Blanc: you see everything, and half of it lies.
> Roc-aux-Sorciers: nothing lies, and you cannot see it.
> Pech Merle: two things tell you where to go, and they disagree.
> Rouffignac: you are on the track, and the train is on time.

**The train never chases.** It leaves the platform on a fixed delay once the tourist is
past it, and it runs at exactly his running speed to the end of the line. Ahead of it and
moving, he is safe for ever. Every step he loses — a rock walked into, a hesitation, a
look at the wall — is lost for good, because the train does not slow down and it does
not stop until the visit does. The only light in the cave apart from his own lamp is its
headlight, and it reaches further up the track than he can see, so it is on the screen
before the train is.

**Everything here is priced in seconds.** Every other level in the chapter kills with a
height or a hole. This one has a clock, so a trap does not have to touch him to finish
him: it only has to cost him more time than he has.

- **The flint in the roof** hangs four pixels above a walking head. Walking under a band
  is nothing; any jump under one is the end. The flint is how the level says *here you may
  not leave the ground*, and no stop board is ever under one.
- **The stop boards** have to be jumped. Walking into one stops him dead, which costs a
  second, which is the whole gap.
- **The check rails.** Four slots of track, all four drawn by the same code, and two of
  them the kind a boot goes into and does not come out of. Nothing kills him there. He
  stands in the track, in one piece, pulling at his boot for as long as the player presses
  to go on (`hiker-held`), and watches the light behind him get bigger. It is the only
  death in the game that takes its time.
- **The track bed.** Twice it is not there any more. The rails are — laid straight across
  the hole the way rails are, because rails hold up a train and not a man. Stepping on
  what is left of the bed gives him a sixth of a second to be somewhere else.
- **The signal.** Once, near the end, a lamp swings out of the roof over the last board
  when he comes up to it, at exactly the height the flint hangs at. It is out for a third
  of a second. The jump he takes without waiting is the jump it is there for; the third
  of a second he spends waiting is the only part of the train's gap he ever spends on
  purpose.

**And then the visit ends.** The track runs out where the real one does, at the edge of
the floor that was dug down under the Great Ceiling, and the train stops with its nose at
the end of the track, which is exactly where a visitor stands to look up. The tourist who
runs off the end onto the lowered floor is safe: the one made thing in the cave is the one
place the train cannot reach. The tourist who does what every visitor does — stops, and
looks up — is where the train stops.

## Deliberate lies

- **The flint in the roof hangs low enough to matter.** The visited galleries of
  Rouffignac are large — some ten metres high — and the roof lowers gradually toward the
  Great Ceiling; nothing on the train route is at head height. The nodules are real, their
  bands are real, and their height over the track is level design.
- **The stop boards, the check rails, the holes in the bed and the signal lamp.** The line
  is maintained, it is inspected, it carries about a kilometre of visitors an hour, and
  none of these is on it. The check rail is a real piece of railway and a boot really does
  go into the slot beside one; there are none in this cave. The signal is invented
  outright and its note says so.
- **The lower galleries are under the track.** They are under the Great Ceiling, which is
  where the shaft is, and not under the middle of the line.
- **The track fills the gallery.** The real galleries are a dozen metres wide with the
  track along one side. Here the walls close on the track so there is nowhere to step off,
  which is what stops the train ever passing you and becoming a wall you follow (pillar 5).
- **The train runs at a walking man's speed and stops where you would stand.** It runs
  slowly and it stops near the Great Ceiling, not at a spot chosen for a joke.
- **Nothing is left lying between the rails** of a train that runs every twenty minutes,
  and nothing is allowed to fall out of the bed under one.
- **The layout**: the galleries are in this order and at these distances because the level
  needs them to be. Spacing and jump distances are level design (`arc.md`, invariants).

## What is true and is doing the work

- The train is real, it dates from 1959, it carries the lighting, and it is on time.
- The flint is real, in bands, and the Magdalenians drew round it.
- The bears are real: claw marks on the walls and ceilings, hollows in the clay.
- The names are real, and they really are scratched across the mammoths. They are drawn
  as a different kind of mark from the bears' — straight, pale, in rows — and never as
  anything you could read (pillar 2).
- The Great Ceiling is real, it is over a shaft, and the floor under it was lowered and the
  hole filled so visitors could stand. The one made floor in the cave is the one place the
  train cannot reach, and that is not a joke the level invented.

## Not in the level

The 1956 affair — Nougier, Robert, Breuil, Blanc, the commission — is the best story the
site has and it has no wordless mechanic. It lives here and in the chapter's error
dossier, and nowhere the player can see.

## Confidence

**Solid.** The train since 1959, carrying the lighting, about a kilometre in; the Great
Ceiling with its sixty-five animals over a shaft, the artist on his back, the hole filled
and the floor lowered; the flint in bands, defining the decorated areas; the bear claw
marks and hollows; Belleforest 1575; graffiti across the mammoth frieze; 1956 and the
war of the mammoths; 158 mammoths, ten rhinos, the two friezes of the Galerie Breuil.

**Discrepant in the sources:** how far in the train goes. The official site says about
one kilometre from the entrance; a tourism page says about two. The level uses one.

**Not verified, so ask before painting:** the order in which the train passes the two
friezes and the bear galleries; how many cars the train has and what colour it is (the
game's is green, and that is a guess); the exact dates on any particular graffito.
