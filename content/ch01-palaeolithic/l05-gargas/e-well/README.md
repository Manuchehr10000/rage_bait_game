# Beat e · the well of the Oubliettes

Twelve tiles where the floor is gone, all the way down. Across it the fitted path: three
concrete slabs on their bearers, the middle one a step up, each railed like the stair, with
a stanchion at each end in the same foot as the stair's. Falling in is `The oubliettes`.

**The middle slab tips.** It is not bearing on anything. A sixth of a second after he lands
on it, it goes off its bearer, the far end first, and it is not under him any more: whoever
is still on it goes down the well with it, not down on it. The first and third slabs are
further apart than a running jump carries, so the middle one has to be touched and left in
the same breath: a jump off it within about a quarter of a second of landing, or none.

**The last slab keeps a boot that is pulled at.** Its foot takes his boot wherever on the
slab he comes down, and six tenths of a second later the slab goes, as the middle one did.
Pressing to go on is pulling at the boot, and so is jumping, and so is pressing anything:
it keeps the boot. Left alone for three tenths of a second, the boot comes free with the
sound of a step and he stands up straight, and there are three tenths of a second left to
jump for the far rim. So the answer is to let go of everything the moment he lands, and
then go. It comes straight after the slab that punished him for not leaving at once, and a
man who does nothing at all goes down with it as well, having seen his boot come free:
which is how the answer is found. It is the only trap in the level that makes him wait,
and it is the last one (pillar 5). The first slab is the one honest slab, and the only place
over the well to stand and look.

The point of the beat is still the lamp. The fastest run that never touches the switch
arrives at the first slab as the flicker starts, so the jump on to the slab that tips is
made in a light that is going on and off on a quarter-second clock, and the lamp runs out
over the well. Short of the far edge of the slab that tips, that is the end: he sits down
in the dark (`The dark`). Past it, the day from the way out is enough, and the lamp can run
out as it likes; the wait on the last slab is past that edge. A run that walked the upper cave
in the dark arrives with a third of the battery and a steady cone. A run that lost more
than about a second and a third on the way, standing, pinned on the ninth step, or looking
at the Camarin by lamplight, does not arrive at all.

**Must be right:** the well is deep and it is in the lower cave; the path is the modern
visit's, made and railed.

**Deliberately wrong:** the well is not on the route and the route does not cross it on
slabs; nothing in the sources says any part of the real path has ever failed anybody, or
that a fitting of it ever caught a boot. The foot and what it does are the level's. The
concrete is Pech Merle's. The day does not reach the well. The name is the real one.

No new art. The slabs are the walkway pieces of Pech Merle and the rail is the stair's,
drawn by the game.
