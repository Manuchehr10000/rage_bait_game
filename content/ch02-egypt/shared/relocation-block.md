## What it is

One block of a temple that has been cut into pieces to be moved. At Abu Simbel in 1964–68 the temples were sawn into 1,036 blocks (plus rock around them), each up to about 30 tonnes, numbered, lifted and reassembled 65 m higher and 210 m back from the water. At Philae in 1972–80 about 40,000 blocks were dismantled and rebuilt on Agilkia. The game paints the number on the face; the designer paints a blank block with saw-cut edges.

## Where it stands in the game

Level 1: six blocks numbered 414–419 over the pit; stepping on 417 lifts them out on cranes. Level 2: blocks 201–206 with cranes above, which do nothing. 16 × 32 each, drawn edge to edge in a row of six.

## Must be right

- The saw cuts: the blocks were cut with hand saws and wire saws, with kerfs a few millimetres wide, and the cut faces are flat and pale against the weathered outer face. Show one weathered face and clean-cut sides.
- Leave the number area (upper third of the face) plain so the painted number reads.
- The stone: Nubian sandstone at Abu Simbel, sandstone at Philae. One block serves both; keep it neutral warm sandstone.

## Deliberately wrong

The cranes and cables the game draws are simplified. Real blocks were lifted by crane onto trucks and moved by road, not hoisted straight up.

## Easter eggs

The Abu Simbel numbers 414–419 are invented. If the designer or a historian finds a published block-numbering photograph, we would happily use real numbers from the facade.

## Sources

- Photographs of the cut facade in the UNESCO archive (reference only, not in repo).

## Confidence

Block count 1,036 and 30 tonnes: well documented. Philae 40,000: commonly cited, exact count varies by source.
