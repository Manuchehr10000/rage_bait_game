# Level 1 · The Great Temple of Abu Simbel

## The site

Two rock-cut temples on the west bank of the Nile in Nubia, about 280 km south of Aswan, cut for Ramesses II between roughly 1264 and 1244 BC. The Great Temple is dedicated to Amun-Ra, Ra-Horakhty, Ptah and the deified Ramesses himself; the Small Temple, 100 m north, to Hathor and Queen Nefertari. The facade of the Great Temple is a pylon shape carved into the cliff, 30 to 33 m high and 35 to 38 m wide as the sources give it, with four seated colossi of the king 20 to 22 m tall. Inside, a hall with eight Osiride pillars leads 65 m into the rock to a sanctuary with four seated statues.

The sand covered it for most of two thousand years. Johann Ludwig Burckhardt saw the tops of the colossi in 1813; Giovanni Belzoni dug his way to the door in 1817. When Lake Nasser began to rise behind the Aswan High Dam, UNESCO led the campaign of 1964–68 that sawed both temples into blocks and rebuilt them some 64 to 65 m higher and 180 to 200 m back from the water (UNESCO gives 64 m and 180 m), inside an artificial hill of concrete domes covered with rock. The reassembly is accurate to within centimetres; the joins are still visible as thin lines if you look.

## Details a teacher will look for

- The colossi wear the **nemes with the double crown** on top, a **uraeus** on the brow and the **false beard**. Cartouches of Ramesses on the chest and upper arms.
- At the legs of each colossus stand small figures of the family, beside the shins and in front between the feet, reaching roughly the king's knee. They differ from statue to statue. From the south: Princesses Bintanath and Nebettawy, in queens' regalia; Queen Nefertari, the king's mother Tuya and Prince Amun-her-khepeshef, at the broken colossus left of the door; Nefertari again with Princess Baketmut and Prince Ramesses, right of the door; Tuya again with Princesses Meritamen and Nefertari, at the northernmost. Queen Nefertari is not at the southernmost colossus.
- Over the door, in a niche, **Ra-Horakhty** stands with a falcon head and sun disc. In the real niche he holds a **user** sceptre and a figure of **Maat**, which with the sun (Ra) spells the king's throne name User-Maat-Ra. That rebus is the single most teacher-pleasing detail on the facade.
- Above the cornice, the **22 baboons**. Below the colossi, a terrace.
- Between the second and third colossi, the doorway, with a small cornice.
- Inside: the hall's **Osiride pillars** show the king wrapped as Osiris with crook and flail. The north wall carries the **Battle of Kadesh** (1274 BC) in sunk relief: the camp, the chariot charge, the king alone against the Hittites.
- In the sanctuary, left to right: **Ptah, Amun-Ra, Ramesses II, Ra-Horakhty**. The order matters.
- The sun reaches the sanctuary over a window of days around each date, not on one day: ten successive days were recorded in October 1959, before the move. Whether the move shifted the dates by a day, and which way, is disputed (see Confidence); UNESCO says the temple was rebuilt in the same orientation.

## Deliberate lies

- The ankh block.
- The fourth head falls on you. No head has fallen since antiquity.
- The baboon's date.
- The blocks go up on cables and the lake comes up in seconds. The real move took four years and the lake rose over a decade.
- The sun beam sweeps in as a wall of light and burns. The real light is a soft rectangle that lasts about twenty minutes.

## Confidence

Dates of construction, the four gods and their order, the 22 baboons, the broken second colossus, the twice-yearly sun in the sanctuary and the relocation of 1964–68 are all well documented.

**Contested, never in the level** (`arc.md`, section 7): the relocation figures (64 m and 180 m by UNESCO, 65 m and 200 m commonly, more than 60 m by Britannica; the campaign as 1963–68 in Britannica); the facade's size (30 × 35 m, or about 33 × 38 m) and the colossi's (20 or 22 m); whether the sun days moved with the temple, and which way; when and why the second colossus broke (soon after completion, or 27 BC, whose earthquake the seismic catalogues question), so say "in antiquity". The per-statue list of family figures comes from secondary summaries that conflict in places (Bintanath and Nebettawy are put at the southernmost by some and the second from the south by others); check against Lepsius before painting names onto anything, and never label them.
